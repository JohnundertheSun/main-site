## A reflection on power, protection, and responsibility in Aruba

Autonomy is often spoken of as a legal status, a constitutional arrangement, or a political achievement. It is something a country either has or does not have. In Aruba, this understanding is deeply embedded in our collective memory. The attainment of Status Aparte in 1986 is widely regarded as the moment in which autonomy became real, formalized, and secured.

Yet the current discussion surrounding the Kingdom Act on Sustainable Public Finances, commonly referred to as HOFA, reveals something more complex. Beneath the legal language of fiscal discipline and financial sustainability lies a deeper and more uncomfortable question: **what does autonomy actually mean in practice?**

Because if autonomy can be limited, conditioned, or gradually transferred without formally being removed, then autonomy is not simply a legal status. It is something else. Something more fragile. Something more alive.

### Autonomy as structure, autonomy as experience

The debate around HOFA has largely been framed in technical terms. Supporters emphasize financial stability, improved credibility, and access to favorable borrowing conditions. Critics point to the structural limitations it imposes on local decision-making and the increased influence of external actors within the Kingdom. ([Aruba News](https://arubanews.ca/rijkswet-hofa-aruba-autonomy/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Both perspectives are valid within their own logic. The law indeed seeks to anchor financial discipline and long-term sustainability. At the same time, it introduces mechanisms that constrain the range of choices available to Aruba’s elected institutions. Budgetary decisions become bounded by externally defined norms, and deviation from these norms can trigger direct intervention. ([eaNews](https://www.eanews.com/analysis-the-brake-from-the-hague-how-the-hofa-kingdom-law-binds-the-hands-of-arubas-parliament/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

But the most important insight lies not in choosing between these positions. It lies in recognizing that autonomy is being redefined in the space between them.

### The subtle shift: from decision to condition

Traditionally, autonomy is understood as the ability to make decisions independently. A parliament debates, decides, and acts. Responsibility follows from that freedom.

Under frameworks like HOFA, this dynamic begins to shift. Decisions are no longer entirely free. They are **conditioned**:

- Deficit limits are predefined,
- Debt thresholds are externally anchored,
- Oversight bodies evaluate compliance before decisions take effect,
- Intervention mechanisms exist if norms are not met.

The formal structure of autonomy remains intact. Parliament still votes. Government still governs. But the _range of_ **_meaningful choices_** narrows.

Autonomy becomes less about deciding what is possible and more about operating within what is permitted. This is not the disappearance of autonomy, but its transformation. Yet such transformations rarely remain neutral. Without clear limits, transparency, and active engagement, conditioned autonomy risks slowly narrowing into compliance, where decisions continue to be made, but no longer fully owned.

### The logic of protection

To understand this transformation, it is important to acknowledge its underlying rationale.

HOFA is not presented as an instrument of control, but as a form of protection:

- Protection against unsustainable debt.
- Protection against financial instability.
- Protection against future crises.

This logic is not unique to Aruba. It reflects a broader pattern in governance, where systems are designed to reduce risk by limiting variability. In doing so, they aim to create stability.

And stability, especially in small and economically vulnerable states, is not a trivial goal.

Yet protection always comes with a condition. It requires the acceptance of limits.

### When protection becomes structure

The critical question is not whether protection is necessary.

The question is: **what happens when protective measures become permanent structures?**

History offers a quiet warning here. Temporary arrangements, introduced during moments of crisis, have a tendency to endure. Financial supervision in the Caribbean parts of the Kingdom, initially framed as transitional, has in some cases remained in place for over a decade. ([The People's Tribune](https://www.thepeoplestribunesxm.com/articles/aruba-faces-new-financial-framework-under-hofa-kingdom-act-promise-and-constraint?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

HOFA risks institutionalizing this dynamic at a deeper level.

By embedding financial constraints within a Kingdom law, the framework becomes difficult to alter unilaterally. Changes require agreement across the Kingdom, effectively placing a portion of Aruba’s policy space outside its own direct control. ([eaNews](https://www.eanews.com/analysis-the-brake-from-the-hague-how-the-hofa-kingdom-law-binds-the-hands-of-arubas-parliament/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This creates a structural reality in which:

- Autonomy is formally preserved.
- But materially constrained.
- And politically mediated beyond the local level.

Again, autonomy does not disappear. It becomes conditional.

### The psychological dimension of autonomy

Up to this point, the discussion remains institutional. It concerns laws, frameworks, and governance structures.

But autonomy is not only institutional. It is also psychological.

It lives in how people relate to power, responsibility, and voice. And here, the impact of conditional autonomy becomes more subtle, but perhaps more profound.

When decisions are consistently framed as constrained, supervised, or predetermined, a shift can occur in collective behavior:

- Questions are asked less often.
- Limits are accepted more quickly.
- Responsibility is deferred more easily.

Silence becomes normalized, not through force, but through adaptation.

This is the quiet risk. Not that autonomy is taken away. But that it is gradually _not exercised_.

### The role of institutions and representation

This dynamic becomes especially relevant when we consider institutions such as trade unions, professional bodies, and representative organizations.

These structures exist precisely to mediate between individuals and systems. They give form to collective voice.

In the context of HOFA, their role becomes more critical, not less. Because if autonomy is increasingly shaped by conditions, then the question is no longer only what the law allows.

It becomes:

- How clearly those conditions are understood.
- How transparently they are communicated.
- And how actively they are engaged.

Representation cannot remain symbolic. It must become active. Otherwise, it risks becoming procedural rather than meaningful.

### Autonomy as responsibility

This brings us to the core insight.

Autonomy is not simply something that is granted or taken away. It is something that is **practiced**.

Legal frameworks can expand or restrict the space within which autonomy operates. But they do not determine whether autonomy is lived. That depends on:

- Whether questions are asked.
- Whether conditions are examined.
- Whether silence is accepted or challenged.
- Whether participation is passive or active.

In this sense, autonomy is less a fixed state and more an ongoing process. A practice of engagement. A willingness to remain present within complexity, rather than retreating into simplicity.

### The real tension

The debate around HOFA is often framed as a binary:

- Stability versus autonomy
- Responsibility versus freedom
- Protection versus independence

But this framing is misleading.

The real tension is not between these values. It is within them, because:

- Stability without autonomy risks dependency.
- Autonomy without responsibility risks instability.
- Protection without limits risks control.
- Freedom without structure risks fragmentation.

The challenge is not to choose one over the other.

It is to maintain a balance that remains alive, responsive, and accountable.

### A living question

Perhaps the most important contribution of the HOFA debate is not the law itself, but the question it forces us to confront.

What does it mean, today, to be autonomous?

Not in theory. Not in law. But in practice.

In daily governance. In institutional behavior. In collective participation.

Because autonomy, once achieved, is not guaranteed. It must be continuously enacted.

### Now what?

If this reflection resonates, the next step is not to take a position immediately, but to begin observing more closely. Where, in your own environment, are decisions presented as fixed when they might be questioned? Where do conditions exist without clarity? And where has silence quietly replaced participation?

Real autonomy begins in these small recognitions. In asking one more question. In seeking one more layer of understanding. In putting something into writing when it would be easier to let it pass.

From there, it becomes a practice.
